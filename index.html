Ship it? #1Made-up numbers, real trap

The test says win. Would you ship it?

New product page. 50/50 split, two weeks.

ControlTreatment
Users6,000 apart503,000497,000
Buyers15,09015,283
Conversion3.000%3.075%
Lift
+2.5%
p-value
0.029significant

The call is D: don't ship yet.

Look at the users, not the buyers. A 50/50 split of 1,000,000 users puts 500,000 in each group, give or take. In 95% of fair tests control lands within ±980 of that. Here it is 3,000 off: 6 standard deviations, a 1 in 507 million event.

That is a sample ratio mismatch (χ² = 36). Somewhere between randomization and the dashboard, one group lost more users than the other. The groups are no longer comparable, so the +2.5% measures the leak as much as the page.

One way it happens: the new page loads slower, and 6,000 treatment visitors leave before the tracking event fires. None of them buy. Count them back in and the lift is +1.28% with p = 0.26: too small to tell from zero.

Why not the others

  • AShip it. p < 0.05, done. The p-value assumes randomization and logging worked. Here they did not.
  • BShip with a 5% holdback. A holdback on the same pipeline inherits the same bias.
  • CRun two more weeks. More data shrinks variance, not bias.

Find the leak, fix it, rerun. It is not rare: about 6% of A/B tests at Microsoft had a sample ratio mismatch (Fabijan et al., KDD 2019).
